What this says

Three parameters moved a lot, and they are the right three. Tooling (+20), the standard library (+17) and performance (+15) are what a user feels day to day. They are also where the two-agent loop works best: one side writes the compiler change, the other verifies it and builds the tooling around it.

What has not moved is structural, not neglected. Concurrency, ecosystem and security sit at 0–3% because they need things a project at this stage cannot manufacture: a package community, a threading runtime, a security model. Raising those numbers means pretending. Better to keep them honest and visible.

The honest ceiling. A language whose compiler is one hand-written NASM file with no runtime will never score well on ecosystem or interop — those assume a large surrounding world. What it can do is score well on performance, tooling, self-sufficiency and the honesty of its error messages. That is the column to compete in, and the last stretch moved exactly those.

Measured state, for the record

  • Tests: 136 passing, including a deterministic codegen gate (3/3) that asserts instruction ceilings and loop shape.
  • Compiler: 14,986 lines of NASM, 359 KB source, building both a Linux ELF and a Windows PE32+ from the same file.
  • IDE: 1,339 lines of NASM, 18 KB binary, verified through a pseudo-terminal harness.
  • Language: 28 keywords, 136 example programs, 10 language packs.
  • Performance: hot loop 13 → 5 instructions; loop_sum 66.96 → 13.3 ms (4.9×); PERF2 added a further ~5% on loop-heavy code.
  • Known open bugs: none outstanding — B8, B9, B10, B11 all fixed and regression-tested.
Scores are judgement calls, not measurements — but every number in "Measured state" is real, and the direction of each score is backed by a specific change that can be pointed at. Where a parameter did not move, it says so.
